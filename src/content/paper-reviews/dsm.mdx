## The question and why it matters

Picture a live interpreter at a conference. She does not wait for the speaker to finish the whole talk; she listens, stays a sentence or so behind, and talks while the speaker keeps going. If she stays too close she guesses wrong; too far and the audience waits.

Computers face the same choice in two jobs:

- **Automatic speech recognition (ASR):** sound in, words out (what Whisper does; see [/paper-reviews/whisper/](/paper-reviews/whisper/)).
- **Text to speech (TTS):** words in, sound out.

Both are **sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq)** problems: one sequence goes in, a different sequence comes out. Most strong systems are **offline**: they swallow the whole input first, then produce the output (Sec 1, p. 1). Whisper reads a 30-second window before writing. That is fine for a podcast transcript, bad for a voice assistant that must answer while you are still talking.

**Streaming** systems produce output while input is still arriving. Earlier streaming methods learn a **policy**: at each moment, a little decision "read more input, or write output now?" (Sec 1, p. 1; Sec 2, p. 2). The paper names two costs: these policies are hard to **batch** (run many users at once on one GPU), and speech-to-text and text-to-speech usually need different model designs (Sec 2, p. 2).

DSM's bet: skip the policy. Line both streams up on one clock before training, then make the output wait a fixed number of ticks. The interpreter's rule becomes "always stay exactly 2.5 seconds behind".

If you have read the Moshi review, this is the dial from [One dial: dialogue, ASR or TTS](/paper-reviews/moshi/#one-dial-dialogue-asr-or-tts) turned into a full method and pushed to state-of-the-art quality. I will not re-explain Moshi's streams, Mimi tokens or the depth transformer here; the [Moshi review](/paper-reviews/moshi/) covers them.

Where we go: [one clock for two streams](#one-clock-for-two-streams), [why the output must wait](#why-the-output-must-wait), [the model](#the-model-one-backbone-many-streams), [DSM-ASR](#dsm-asr-listening-with-a-fixed-lag), [DSM-TTS](#dsm-tts-speaking-with-a-fixed-lag), then [What the evidence shows](#what-the-evidence-shows).

## The pieces, before the details

- **Stream:** a row of tokens, one token per tick of a shared clock.
- **Frame:** one tick. DSM uses Mimi's clock, **12.5** ticks per second, so one frame is **80 ms** (Sec 3.1, p. 4; Sec 3.2, p. 5).
- **Delay** $\tau$: how many frames the output row is shifted to the right of the input row (Sec 3, p. 4, Eq 7).
- **PAD** and **WORD:** special text tokens. PAD means "no new word at this tick"; WORD means "a word starts here" (Sec 3.1, p. 5).
- **Backbone:** one decoder-only Transformer that reads all streams at each tick (Sec 3, p. 4).

## One clock for two streams

### Turning timestamps into a grid

Speech datasets usually say "this sentence goes with this clip", not where each word sits (Sec 3.2, p. 5). DSM needs word-level times. The authors get them from **whisper-timestamped**, a wrapper around Whisper that outputs words with start times (Sec 3.2, p. 5; Sec 4.3.1, p. 9 says it wraps Whisper Medium).

Then each word is placed on the grid with one rule (Sec 3.1, p. 5):

$$
i = \lfloor s \cdot f_r \rfloor
$$

- $s$: the time in seconds where the word starts.
- $f_r$: frames per second, here $12.5$.
- $s \cdot f_r$: how many frames have gone by at that moment (can be a fraction).
- $\lfloor \cdot \rfloor$: "floor", round down to a whole number.
- $i$: the index of the frame where the word is placed.

At frame $i$ goes the WORD token, at $i+1$ the word's first text token, at $i+2$ the second, and so on. Every unused cell gets PAD (Sec 3.1, p. 5).

**Intuition:** a music box drum. Time runs around the drum in fixed slots; each word is a pin pushed into the slot where it starts.

**Worked example:** "Alan" starts at $s = 0.35$ s: $0.35 \cdot 12.5 = 4.375$, floor gives $i = 4$. WORD at frame 4, "Alan" at frame 5. "Turing" starts at $s = 0.80$: $0.80 \cdot 12.5 = 10$, so WORD at frame 10. Frames 0 to 3, 6 to 9 are PAD. (Times are illustrative, not from the paper.)

### Fixing the transcripts

Two cleanup steps for fine-tuning data that has human transcripts but no word times (Sec 3.2, p. 5):

1. A **300M**-parameter prefix language model rewrites transcripts into a consistent style of capitals and punctuation.
2. Whisper makes its own transcript with times; **dynamic time warping (DTW)**, an algorithm that finds the best stretch-and-match between two sequences, lines the human words up with Whisper's timed words, so the human words inherit times.

## Why the output must wait

### The coin trap

The authors give a neat counter-example (Sec 3, p. 5). Flip fair coins $X_1, X_2, \dots$ (each 0 or 1). The answer stream is

$$
Y_t = X_t \oplus X_{t+1}
$$

- $X_t$: the coin at tick $t$.
- $X_{t+1}$: the next coin.
- $\oplus$: XOR, "1 if the two differ, 0 if they match".
- $Y_t$: the answer the model must write at tick $t$.

**Intuition:** the answer at each tick depends on a coin that has not landed yet. A model forced to answer at tick $t$ can only guess, 50/50.

**Worked example:** coins $X = (0, 1)$. The true $Y_1 = 0 \oplus 1 = 1$, always. A model at tick 1 has only seen $X_1 = 0$, so it outputs 1 with probability one half. Its answers are wrong half the time even though it was trained perfectly (Sec 3, p. 5).

### The fix: shift the output right

So DSM lets the output peek $\tau$ frames ahead in the input (Sec 3, p. 5, Eq 7):

$$
q_\tau(y \mid X_{\le t+\tau}, Y_{<t}) \approx P[Y_t = y \mid X_{\le t+\tau}, Y_{<t}]
$$

- $Y_t$: the output token at tick $t$; $y$ is one candidate value.
- $X_{\le t+\tau}$: all input tokens up to $\tau$ ticks after $t$.
- $Y_{<t}$: the outputs already written.
- $P[\cdot]$: the true probability; $q_\tau$: the model's estimate.
- $\approx$: the model is trained to match it.

**Intuition:** the interpreter stays $\tau$ ticks behind, so she has heard a little of what comes next before she commits.

**Worked example:** in the coin trap with $\tau = 1$, at tick 1 the model sees $X_1 = 0$ and $X_2 = 1$, so it writes $Y_1 = 1$ correctly every time. With $\tau = 0$ it is 50/50.

Two extremes (Sec 3, p. 5): $\tau = 0$ gives the trap; $\tau = T$ (the whole length) is just offline seq2seq again. Real speech sits in between: a name might only become clear several words later, so a bigger $\tau$ buys accuracy and costs latency.

## The model: one backbone, many streams

At each tick the backbone gets one token from each stream, looks each up in its own embedding table, **adds** the vectors, and runs one step (Sec 3, p. 4; Fig 2, p. 5). A **sampler** then produces the output token for that tick. Loss is only computed on the output stream; input tokens are fed in but not graded (Sec 3, p. 4).

- For text out, the sampler is one linear layer.
- For audio out, it is a small **RQ-Transformer** that writes Mimi's codebooks one after another inside the tick (Sec 3.3, p. 6). That is Moshi's depth transformer; see the [Moshi review](/paper-reviews/moshi/).
- The paper notes it could be a flow or diffusion head for continuous outputs (Sec 3, p. 4), which is the CALM direction explained in [/blog/pocket-tts-calm-explained/](/blog/pocket-tts-calm-explained/).

Because every user's input arrives at the same 12.5 ticks per second, many users can share one GPU step: one forward pass, one tick for everyone. That is the batching claim (Sec 1, p. 2), measured in [Batching and speed](#batching-and-speed).

Sizes (Sec 4.1, p. 7): DSM-ASR backbone **2.6B** parameters, 48 layers, 2048 wide, text vocabulary **4000**, English only. DSM-TTS backbone **1B**, 16 layers, **1.8B** total with the codebook sampler, vocabulary **8000**, English and French.

## DSM-ASR: listening with a fixed lag

Input $X$: Mimi audio tokens. Output $Y$: the padded text grid (Sec 3.2, p. 5). The model is graded on PAD and WORD too, so it learns where words begin. Shift its WORD positions back by $\tau$ and you get word timestamps at **80 ms** resolution (Sec 3.2, p. 5).

### Choosing the delay at run time

Training one model per delay is wasteful, so DSM-ASR is also trained with a random delay per example, sampled in **0.25 to 4 s**, and told the delay through a **cosine embedding** (the same sine/cosine position code Transformers use) added to its inputs (Sec 3.2, p. 5; Sec 4.1, p. 7). The default evaluated model uses a fixed **2.5 s** delay (Sec 4.3, p. 8).

## DSM-TTS: speaking with a fixed lag

Swap roles: text in, Mimi audio out, audio delayed (Sec 3.3, p. 6). The paper's stated TTS delay is inconsistent: **1.28 s (16 steps)** in Sec 3.3 (p. 6), **1.6 s, or 16 steps** in Sec 4.1 (p. 7), and **2 s, or 25 steps** in App I (p. 22). At 12.5 Hz, 16 steps is 1.28 s and 25 steps is 2 s. The paper does not say which one the released model uses.

### The action stream: when to feed the next word

At training time the padded text is known. At run time you only have plain text: nobody tells you how many PADs go between words. So the model gets an extra output, the **action stream**, which predicts "the next input should be a WORD now" (Sec 3.3, p. 6). When it fires, the system feeds the next word's tokens over the following ticks; until then it feeds PAD (Fig 3, p. 5). The model chooses its own speaking rhythm.

### The lookahead stream

The action stream decides where words go but cannot see what they are. A second text stream feeds word $m_{i+l}$ while the main stream feeds $m_i$, with $l = 2$ (Sec 3.3, p. 6; Sec 4.1, p. 7). So when deciding the pause before word 5, the model already knows word 7.

### Voices, turns and guidance

- **Speakers:** up to **5** speaker embeddings, each from a **10 s** clip, fed through cross-attention; the speaker encoder starts from Mimi's encoder (Sec 3.3, p. 6).
- **Turns:** special MAIN and OTHER tokens mark who speaks next, so you can script a two-voice dialogue (Sec 3.3, p. 6).
- **Classifier-free guidance (CFG)** (Sec 3.3, p. 6, Eq 8):

$$
l_{t,q} = l^{\emptyset}_{t,q} + \alpha \left( l^{\text{text,speaker}}_{t,q} - l^{\emptyset}_{t,q} \right)
$$

- $l_{t,q}$: the final score (logit) for codebook $q$ at tick $t$.
- $l^{\emptyset}_{t,q}$: the score when the model is given no text and no speaker.
- $l^{\text{text,speaker}}_{t,q}$: the score with text and speaker.
- $\alpha$: guidance strength, $\alpha \ge 1$; default **2**.
- The bracket: "how much does knowing the text and voice change my mind".

**Intuition:** take the direction from "mumbling" to "reading this text in this voice" and walk past it, to exaggerate it.

**Worked example:** for one token, $l^{\emptyset} = 1$ and $l^{\text{text,speaker}} = 3$. With $\alpha = 2$: $1 + 2 \cdot (3 - 1) = 5$. With $\alpha = 1$: $1 + 1 \cdot 2 = 3$, the plain conditioned score.

Training drops speaker and text independently **20%** of the time so the unconditioned score exists (Sec 3.3, p. 6). For the public release they then distil CFG into the model (fine-tuned **2400** updates with $\alpha$ as an input, $\alpha \in \{1, 1.5, \dots, 4\}$) because the speaker encoder is kept closed for safety, which rules out the unconditioned pass (Sec 4.2, pp. 7 to 8).

## Training stages and data

- **Pretraining** on **2.5 million hours** of public English and French audio transcribed by whisper-timestamped (Sec 4.2, p. 7). For ASR the authors call this "hard distillation" of whisper-timestamped.
- **DSM-ASR:** 90 s segments, **1.6M** steps on 48 H100s; then **28k hours** of human-transcribed public data for 100k updates; then a long-form mixture for 25k updates (Sec 4.2, p. 7).
- **DSM-TTS:** 150 s extracts, 750k updates, batch 64, 32 H100s (Sec 4.2, p. 7).
- A **public-data-only** DSM-TTS with a **300M** backbone on **88k hours** is provided for reproducibility (Sec 4.4.1, p. 11).

## What the evidence shows

Metrics: **word error rate (WER)** is words wrong per 100 words; for TTS an ASR model transcribes the output first. See [/blog/speech-evaluation-metrics/](/blog/speech-evaluation-metrics/). **MUSHRA** is a 1 to 100 listening score; **Elo** turns pairwise "which sounds more like the target speaker" votes into a rating; **RTF (real-time factor)** here is seconds of output per second of compute, so above 1 means faster than real time (Table 6 caption, p. 11).

### Time to first audio

App I (pp. 22 to 23) gives batch-1 latency as

$$
(\tau f_r - 1)\, d_b + (1 + 2)(d_b + d_q)
$$

- $\tau f_r$: the delay in ticks (they use $\tau = 2$ s, so 25 ticks).
- $d_b$: time for one backbone step, **5 ms**.
- $d_q$: time for the codebook sampler in one tick, **25 ms**.
- First term: ticks where only the backbone runs, because no audio is due yet.
- $(1 + 2)$: the first audio tick plus Moshi's **2**-step acoustic delay between the first codebook and the rest.

**Intuition:** warm-up ticks are cheap (backbone only); real audio ticks are expensive (backbone plus sampler).

**Worked example:** $(25 - 1) \cdot 5 + 3 \cdot (5 + 25) = 120 + 90 = 210$ ms. Table 6 reports **150** ms. With 16 ticks: $15 \cdot 5 + 90 = 165$ ms, still not 150. As printed, the formula and Table 6 do not agree.

### Other findings

- Mimi round-trip with 32 codebooks removes common watermarks: Perth detection **100% to 0%**, AudioSeal **100% to 45.2%** (App K, Table 13, p. 23).
- Fewer than **24** Mimi codebooks hurt ASR WER; more do not help (App F, p. 21).

## Where it breaks

- **Needs aligned data.** The authors name this as the main limitation: aligned domains reduce the gold-standard data usable for training (Sec 5, p. 11).
- **Distilled from Whisper.** Pretraining labels are Whisper medium's; timing quality sits below CrisperWhisper (Table 12, p. 21).
- **Quality gap in TTS.** Lower MUSHRA than Chatterbox and ElevenLabs Flash (Table 5, p. 10).
- **Closed voice encoder.** The best TTS's speaker encoder is not released; users get precomputed embeddings (Sec 4.2, p. 8; Societal impact, p. 12).
- **Internal inconsistencies** around the TTS delay and latency formula, noted above.

## Toy experiments

All run in the browser with fixed seeds and no trained model.

1. **Frame placement** (WordsOnTheClock): six words at toy times 0.35, 0.80, 1.30, 1.55, 1.70, 2.10 s; frame indices 4, 10, 16, 19, 21, 26.
2. **Coin trap** (XorCoinTrap): 16 coins, seed 7; expected accuracy 50% at $\tau = 0$, 100% at $\tau \ge 1$.
3. **Delay vs ambiguity** (StreamShiftLab): clue offsets $[0, 0, 1, 3, 0, 6, 2, 10]$ frames; accuracy 3/8 at $\tau = 0$, 5/8 at 2, 6/8 at 3, 7/8 at 6, 8/8 at 10.
4. **CFG** (GuidanceMixer): the two 6-token logit vectors above; $\alpha$ from 1 to 4.
5. **First audio** (FirstAudioTimer): App I preset gives 210 ms.

## My interpretation

DSM is less a new model than a new place to put the hard part. Learned read/write policies are replaced by a forced aligner run once, offline. Once the data sits on one clock, a plain decoder with plain attention does both directions, batches like a language model, and runs for hours. The 400-stream number is what a production team would care about most.

The weakness is in the same move. Every training example needs word timestamps, and for English those come almost for free from whisper-timestamped. The ASR student even beats its teacher (7.1 vs 8.1), which says label noise in timing and text is tolerable at 2.5 million hours.

**For Khmer** (see [/blog/khmer-tts-fine-tuning/](/blog/khmer-tts-fine-tuning/)):
- Khmer writes words without spaces, so "word start time" first needs a word segmenter, then an aligner that has seen Khmer. Errors in either shift WORD tokens.
- Whisper's Khmer is weak, so the DSM trick of distilling Whisper's timestamps would distil its mistakes too. A CTC (connectionist temporal classification) forced aligner trained on Khmer is the likelier route.
- The ablation suggests where to spend effort: without lookahead, English WER doubles (1.60% to 3.51%, Table 15, p. 25). Khmer's long, stacked syllables may need a larger lookahead $l$, a cheap knob to try.
- The public-data 300M model (Table 7, p. 11) is the realistic scale for free/local compute, not the 1.8B one.

## Open questions

- How small can $\tau$ go for TTS before rhythm breaks, and does delay conditioning (shown only for ASR) work for TTS too?
- How sensitive is DSM to timestamp error? A test that jitters WORD positions by plus or minus 1 to 3 frames would answer it.
- Which TTS delay and latency numbers are correct (1.28, 1.6 or 2 s; 150 or 210 ms)?
- Could a Moshi-style random negative delay let one checkpoint do ASR and TTS together?
- For languages without spaces, is a subword or syllable "WORD" marker enough?
