**Question: how do you decide whether a stage of a data pipeline was worth what it removed?**

Train small models that differ only in their data, at a fixed token budget close to the one you intend, and compare them on the capabilities you care about; then read samples of what the stage removed and count how many times the model will have to repeat what is left. Statistics about the data cannot answer the question, because every stage changes **which** text the model sees and **how often**, not just how much there is. A filter that removes spam also removes something else, and a strict filter that helps at one budget can force heavy repetition at a larger one.

The tokens in the [previous posts](/blog/model-development-small-training-run/) simply arrived. For most open models they come from the public web, and the corpus *is* the objective: the training loss averages over its tokens, so whatever is common in it, the model is pushed hardest to predict. We use [FineWeb](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17557) as the running example because its authors published the pipeline and the experiments behind each choice; the [FineWeb review](/paper-reviews/fineweb/) weighs that evidence in detail.

## What each stage changes

| Stage | What it changes | The decision it hides |
|---|---|---|
| **Extract** text from HTML | Drops menus, cookie banners and footers that repeat across millions of pages | Which extractor; Common Crawl's own plain text keeps too much boilerplate |
| **Identify the language** | Keeps documents whose top label is a wanted language | One score decides the fate of code-switched pages and small languages |
| **Heuristic filters** | Removes lists, link farms and non-prose by simple statistics | Thresholds also remove poetry, instructions and code |
| **Deduplicate** | Removes exact and near copies, changing the **mixture** as well as the size | How similar counts as a copy, and across which crawls |
| **Model-based quality filter** | Keeps documents a classifier scores highly | Whose preferences the labels encode, and which topics shrink |
| **Decontaminate and remove personal data** | Removes evaluation items and patterns like emails | What overlap search cannot see |

At FineWeb's scale, 96 Common Crawl snapshots came to roughly **36 trillion** tokens after the first filters, **20 trillion** after deduplication and **15 trillion** after the rest (FineWeb, §§3.3–3.7): less than half survived. Keep, for every stage, the counts in and out and the removed documents with their reason, plus provenance (FineWeb records each document's URL, snapshot and crawl date), so you can inspect a stage, honour a removal request or rebuild a snapshot. Licensing is a separate question: FineWeb is released under ODC-By 1.0 and its use is also subject to Common Crawl's terms (Appendix B).

Three stages carry decisions worth seeing on an example.

**Extraction.** FineWeb found Common Crawl's plain-text WET files "retained too much boilerplate and menu text" and extracted from raw HTML with trafilatura instead, which trained clearly better models (§3.2, Fig. 1). DCLM measured the same: 24.1 and 24.5 on its core score with two custom extractors against 20.7 for WET text ([DCLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11794), §4.2, Table 3). Extraction is also the most expensive stage to redo, so get it right first.

**Language identification.** FineWeb keeps a document if a fastText classifier says English with probability at least **0.65** (§3.3). Running that filter (datatrove 0.10.1) on three short pages about Angkor Wat: the English page scores English 0.998 and is kept; a half-Khmer, half-English page scores Khmer 0.852 and is removed, English half included; the Khmer page scores Khmer 0.998 and is removed. Correct for an English corpus, but coverage of mixed text hangs on one score, and a multilingual corpus needs per-language thresholds and checks that small languages survive.

**Heuristic filters.** A page of five lines, four of them like "Cheap flights Rome", has 1 of 5 lines ending in punctuation (0.2, which passes FineWeb's 0.12 minimum) but 4 of 5 lines of at most 30 characters (0.8, above the 0.67 maximum), so FineWeb's quality filter removes it for `short_line_ratio`. FineWeb chose thresholds by comparing statistics between better and worse versions of a snapshot and then training models (§3.6). The trade-off shows in how much a rule removes: C4's rule dropping every line without terminal punctuation removes about **30%** of tokens, FineWeb's looser rule about **10%** (§§3.5–3.6).

## Deduplication changes the mixture

A document copied a thousand times is trained on a thousand times, so it carries a thousand times the weight in the loss and is more likely to be memorized. Exact copies are found by hashing normalized text. **Near-duplicates** are found by describing each document by its overlapping five-word sequences and measuring the **Jaccard similarity**, shared sequences over distinct sequences: changing one word of a 100-word document changes 5 sequences, leaving 91 of 101 shared, $J \approx 0.90$. Comparing every pair of billions of documents is impossible, so pipelines use **MinHash**, a short signature per document whose agreement rate estimates Jaccard similarity, and bucket the signatures to find candidate pairs without comparing all of them. The settings decide what counts as a copy: FineWeb's catch a pair at similarity 0.75 about 77% of the time and at 0.85 about 99% (Appendix E.1).

The decision that matters most is **how widely** to deduplicate. FineWeb first deduplicated across all 96 snapshots, newest first. Old snapshots lost up to 90% of their data, and the result trained a model barely better than no deduplication (§3.4, Fig. 3). In the 2013 snapshot, the 10% that survived trained a **worse** model than the 90% removed, and the survivors had more ads, keyword lists and badly formatted text (Fig. 4). Deduplicating each snapshot separately worked better (Fig. 5).

The toy is not a law: plenty of copied text is junk (CS336's data lecture shows one product description repeated 61,036 times in C4). FineWeb's hypothesis is that most of the benefit comes from removing very large clusters of copies, while removing small clusters can hurt (§3.4). Judge deduplication by training on what remains.

**Model-based filters** push the mixture further. FineWeb-Edu had Llama-3-70B-Instruct score 460,000 pages for educational value, trained a small classifier on those scores and kept documents scoring at least 3: 1.3 trillion of 15 trillion tokens (§4). It raised MMLU from 33% to 37% and ARC from 46% to 57% in FineWeb's experiments, and also shifted topics towards education and history and away from business and entertainment (§4.1–4.2). That is either what you want or a coverage loss you did not intend.

## Judge a stage at your training budget

FineWeb compares data variants by training 1.71-billion-parameter models with the same architecture and number of training tokens, two seeds each, on benchmarks chosen for a stable signal at that scale (§3.1). The comparison is always at a fixed budget, and the right answer depends on the budget.

**Try it: predict token exposure.** You will train for **100 billion** tokens. After basic filtering you have **300 billion**. A quality classifier offers two thresholds: keep the top 30% (90 billion tokens) or the top 10% (30 billion). How many times will the model see each kept document? Now change the budget to **1 trillion** tokens.

**Answer.** At 100 billion tokens, the 30% threshold gives 100 / 90 ≈ **1.1** passes and the 10% threshold 100 / 30 ≈ **3.3**. At 1 trillion tokens they give about 11 and 33 passes, and even the unfiltered 300 billion would be seen 3.3 times. At a fixed budget, a smaller retained corpus means more repeated exposure: the stricter filter triples it at either budget, and at 1 trillion tokens that is 33 passes.

The pass count does not say which option trains the better model. That depends on how much better the kept documents are and how fast repeated data loses value on your data at your scale. One study found that, for its models (up to 9 billion parameters) and data, up to about 4 passes changed the final loss negligibly compared with fresh data, and further repetition then lost value sharply ([Muennighoff et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16264), abstract and Fig. 1). That is a prior from one experiment, not a limit. CS336's data lecture puts the trade-off plainly: there is no single best filtering threshold; longer training wants more data, shorter training less but better data. The [next post](/blog/model-development-data-mixtures/) turns this into sampling weights across sources.

FineWeb's own answers hold for its setting: English Common Crawl, 1.71-billion-parameter models, its benchmark suite, and budgets of 28 billion tokens for filter ablations and 350 billion for deduplication. They are evidence at those budgets, not at budgets where its filtered sets would be repeated many times.

## Contamination and personal data

**Contamination** is training-data exposure to evaluation **items, their answers or solutions, or close derivatives** such as paraphrases and translations. It is not learning facts a benchmark also tests. For the item *"Which gas do plants absorb from the air? (A) oxygen (B) carbon dioxide … Answer: B"*, a textbook sentence about photosynthesis is general knowledge; a quiz site copying the item and its answer key is contamination; the same item reworded is contamination too.

The usual check searches the corpus for overlapping word sequences. The quiz page shares all 6 of the question's three-word sequences and is caught. The textbook sentence and the reworded item each share exactly 1, *from the air*: overlap cannot tell general knowledge from a derivative. Paraphrases and translations need semantic search and judgement, and the most reliable protection is keeping your own evaluation data out of the pipeline from the start. The FineWeb paper does not describe a benchmark decontamination step, so if you train on it and evaluate on public benchmarks, that check is yours.

**Personal data.** FineWeb replaces email addresses and public IP addresses with fixed placeholders by regular expression (§3.7); datatrove's `PIIFormatter` on a toy sentence replaces the email and IP and leaves the name "Jane Doe" untouched. Pattern-based removal is cheap and narrow; stronger protection needs entity detection, an audit of what it misses, and provenance for removal requests.

## What goes wrong, and the check

| Mistake | How it shows up | Check |
|---|---|---|
| A filter removes a domain or language you need | The model is weak there; removed samples contain good text | Read each stage's removals per domain and language |
| A stage judged at the wrong budget | A filter that helped small ablations hurts a long run through repetition | Compute passes at the real budget; re-test near it |
| Evaluation items in the corpus | Strong public-benchmark scores, weak results on fresh questions | Overlap search plus semantic search and a private held-out set |

## Try it yourself

- **Filter.** A 10-line page has 3 lines ending in punctuation and 8 short lines. The punctuation fraction 0.3 passes, but the short-line fraction 0.8 exceeds 0.67, so the page is **removed**: one rule is enough.
- **Budget.** A filter leaves 50 billion tokens and you train for 200 billion: about **4 passes**; at 1 trillion, **20**. At that budget, compare the strict filter against a broader one before committing.
- **Contamination.** A forum post quotes a benchmark question in full with its answer: **contamination**, though the facts are general knowledge.
