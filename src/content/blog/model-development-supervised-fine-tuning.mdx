**Question: in supervised fine-tuning, which tokens should carry the loss, and why does that choice change the model?**

SFT is the same next-token loss as pre-training, applied to conversations rendered through a **chat template**; what is new is which tokens are trained. Training only on the assistant's tokens, including the end-of-text token, teaches the model to answer and to stop, rather than to imitate users; in a short example conversation that is 8 of 25 tokens. How those per-token losses are averaged matters too: averaging per sequence can give a short reply's tokens 5.5 times the weight of a long reply's. The rendered template must match exactly at training and inference time, and the data mix decides which skills appear: in Tülu 3, removing math data cut GSM8K from 76.2 to 64.1.

A base model has learned to continue text, not to treat a question as a request; asked "What is the capital of France?", it may continue with another question. SFT teaches the missing behaviour from examples. You need only the [first post](/blog/model-development-one-update/) and the [base-checkpoint post](/blog/model-development-base-checkpoint/) to follow this one.

## A conversation becomes text: the chat template

A model reads one sequence of tokens, so a conversation must be flattened into one. A **chat template** does it: it wraps each message in role markers and ends each assistant turn with a stop token. The released Tülu 3 tokenizer renders a two-message conversation as

```text
<|user|>
What is 2+3?
<|assistant|>
2+3 = 5.<|end_of_text|>
```

Three consequences follow:

- **The template is part of the model.** It is learned during fine-tuning, so the same template must be used at inference. A different one, or none, puts the model in an unfamiliar format. Tülu 3 compared five templates and found differences of about 1.4 points on its average (§4.3.1, Table 13): the choice matters less than consistency.
- **Role markers are often special tokens,** added to the vocabulary for fine-tuning. Their embeddings start untrained, as the [tokenization post](/blog/model-development-tokenization/#when-text-and-tokenizer-disagree) noted.
- **The stop token is what makes the model stop.** At inference, generation ends when the model produces `<|end_of_text|>`. It only learns to do that if the stop token is among the trained tokens.

## Which tokens are trained: the loss mask

Usually the model is trained only to produce the assistant's replies, not to predict the user's messages. The labels for every other token are set to −100, the ignore value from the first post, so they contribute no loss. For the conversation above, rendering it with the Tülu 3 tokenizer gives 25 tokens; the first 17 (the user's message and the `<|assistant|>` header) are masked, and the 8 tokens of `2+3 = 5.<|end_of_text|>` are trained. open-instruct, the code behind Tülu 3, builds exactly this kind of mask by rendering each prefix of the conversation and masking every non-assistant span, including the header that announces the assistant's turn.

Two points from the first post carry over directly:

- **Masked tokens still shape the gradient.** A masked user message receives no direct loss, but the assistant's tokens attend to it, so its representations are still trained through them. The model learns to *use* the prompt, just not to predict it.
- **Masking prompts is a default, not a law.** Shi et al. (2024) found that also training on the instruction tokens can help in many settings, especially when instructions are long and answers short, or when there are few examples, which they attribute to reduced overfitting ([Shi et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14394), abstract).

**Try it: trace the labels.** A conversation has a system message of 10 tokens, a user message of 20, an assistant reply of 30, another user message of 15 and a final assistant reply of 40. Each assistant reply ends with one stop token. How many tokens are trained with assistant-only masking? And if only the last turn is trained?

**Answer.** All assistant turns: $(30 + 1) + (40 + 1) =$ **72** tokens, the replies and their stop tokens; the 45 tokens of system and user messages and every role header are masked. Last turn only: **41**. Training only on the last turn treats earlier replies as context, which is useful when they came from a weaker model; training on all turns uses more of the data.

## How the loss is averaged

Conversations have very different reply lengths, so averaging matters as much here as it did for packed pre-training rows. Suppose one conversation has a 100-token reply with mean loss 1.0 and another a 10-token reply with mean loss 2.0:

- **Per token:** $(100 \times 1.0 + 10 \times 2.0)/110 \approx 1.09$; every trained token weighs $1/110$.
- **Per sequence**, averaging each conversation first: $(1.0 + 2.0)/2 = 1.5$; each token of the short reply weighs $1/20$, **5.5 times** as much.

The Tülu 3 team ran into exactly this. Their models trained on GPUs underperformed others, and the cause was loss aggregation in Hugging Face Transformers: with gradient accumulation each sample was averaged separately, so samples, not tokens, were weighted equally, and changing the accumulation steps changed the weighting. They switched to a summed loss, which weights every token equally, retuned the learning rate, and found it generally better (§4.3.2). It is the same trap as mean-then-divide accumulation in the [first post](/blog/model-development-one-update/#gradient-accumulation-must-keep-the-token-mean) and per-rank averaging in the [data-parallelism post](/blog/model-development-data-parallelism/#the-normalization-trap).

## What the data teaches

The mechanism is simple; the data is where the work is. Tülu 3 is unusually informative because it published ablations of its own mix for Llama 3.1 8B (§4.2, Table 10):

| Removed from the mix | Average | Most affected |
|---|---|---|
| nothing (full mix) | 60.1 | — |
| WildChat (diverse real conversations) | 58.9 | AlpacaEval 2: 12.4 → 7.5 |
| safety data | 58.0 | safety: 93.1 → 74.7, others nearly unchanged |
| math data | 58.2 | GSM8K: 76.2 → 64.1; MATH: 31.5 → 23.5 |

Three lessons generalize:

- **Skills come from targeted data.** Removing math examples cost 12 points on GSM8K; the rest of the mix could not compensate.
- **Some behaviours are nearly independent.** Safety data changed the safety score a lot and other skills little. Adding contrastive prompts, requests that look risky but are fine, helped avoid over-refusal.
- **Diversity of real requests matters** for open-ended conversation quality, where curated tasks alone fall short.

Fine-tuning is short and sensitive. Tülu 3's 8B model trained for 2 epochs at a learning rate of $5 \times 10^{-6}$, with an effective batch of 128 sequences of up to 4,096 tokens (Table 11). Changing only the random seed moved the average between 59.8 and 59.9, and averaging the weights of two runs ("model soup") gave 60.2, not reliably better than the best single run (Table 14). Differences smaller than a few tenths of a point are within noise.

The base model matters a great deal. The same SFT mix gave 31.5 on MATH from Llama 3.1 8B but 56.4 from Qwen 2.5 Math 7B, a model whose pre-training emphasized mathematics (Table 12). In Tülu 3's setting, a short SFT run mostly drew out and formatted what pre-training had learned; that is not a hard ceiling, since larger or longer fine-tuning, distillation from a stronger model and later RL can all add capability, but it is why choosing the base model is usually the biggest decision.


## What can go wrong

- **Template mismatch** at inference: fluent but oddly formatted or never-ending output. Check that serving uses the exact training template.
- **Stop token not trained,** for example because it was masked or truncated: the model runs on past its answer.
- **Weighting by sequence by accident:** results change when gradient accumulation or the number of GPUs changes. Sum the loss and divide by the global token count.
- **Contaminated chat data:** real chat logs contain benchmark questions. Tülu 3 found public datasets such as ShareGPT, WildChat and LMSys Chat likely to overlap with test sets and decontaminated its mix (§3.2; App. B.2), at a small cost in measured scores.
