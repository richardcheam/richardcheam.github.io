This series used language models as its teaching vehicle because text is where the methods were developed and are easiest to see. But the same ideas now train image, speech and video models, and systems that handle several at once. This post maps what carries over and what does not, without trying to cover each modality in depth; the site's [speech posts](/blog/mimi-codec-explained/) and [Foundations series](/blog/foundations-diffusion-flow/) go further.

## What carries over almost unchanged

- **The Transformer** works on any sequence of vectors. Once an image or a sound is a sequence, the [decoder architecture](/blog/model-development-decoder-architecture/) applies with few changes.
- **Training mechanics:** the [training update](/blog/model-development-one-update/), learning-rate schedules, mixed precision, checkpoints and the diagnosis of a [training run](/blog/model-development-small-training-run/) are the same.
- **Scale:** [data, tensor and pipeline parallelism](/blog/model-development-model-parallelism/) and [sharding](/blog/model-development-sharding-recomputation/) are indifferent to what the tokens mean.
- **Data discipline:** filtering, deduplication, mixture weights and decontamination, from the [corpus](/blog/model-development-pretraining-corpus/) and [mixtures](/blog/model-development-data-mixtures/) posts, apply to images and audio, though the filters differ.
- **Scaling laws** in the same form, fitted separately for each modality and objective.
- **Post-training and evaluation habits:** SFT on demonstrations, preference learning, held-out evaluations and suspicion of single benchmarks.

## What changes: turning the signal into tokens

Text arrives as discrete symbols. Images and sound are continuous and much larger, so the first question for any modality is how to turn it into a sequence the model can handle, and how long that sequence is.

**Images.** The Vision Transformer cuts an image into fixed-size patches, 16 × 16 pixels, flattens each into a vector and treats the patches as a sequence ([Dosovitskiy et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11929)). A 224 × 224 image becomes **196** patches; a 1,024 × 1,024 image becomes 4,096, and because attention cost grows with the square of sequence length, resolution is expensive. Earlier vision models used [convolutions](/blog/foundations-convolutions/), which build in the assumption that nearby pixels matter together; ViT drops that assumption and needs more data to compensate, but then scales well.

**Speech.** Audio is sampled thousands of times per second (24,000 for the codecs on this site), far too long a sequence to model directly. A **neural audio codec** compresses it: Mimi turns speech into 12.5 frames per second, and with [residual vector quantization](/blog/residual-vector-quantization/) each frame becomes 8 discrete codes in Moshi's setting: **100 codec symbols per second**, but only **12.5 positions per second** for Moshi's main (temporal) transformer, because a small depth transformer generates the 8 codebooks within each frame ([Mimi post](/blog/mimi-codec-explained/); [Moshi review](/paper-reviews/moshi/)). Other systems skip discrete tokens and model continuous latents, as [Pocket TTS](/blog/pocket-tts-calm-explained/) does.

The token rate decides cost, exactly as tokenizer premiums did for languages in the [tokenization post](/blog/model-development-tokenization/#what-the-same-text-costs).

**Try it: what fits in a context?** An 8,192-token context holds how many 224 × 224 images at one token per patch? How many seconds of audio if the 100 codec symbols per second were flattened into one sequence, and how many as Moshi actually lays them out, 12.5 positions per second? How much English text, at about 1.3 tokens per word?

**Answer.** $8{,}192 / 196 \approx$ **41 images**; about **6,300 words** of text. For audio, a hypothetical flattened stream of all 100 symbols per second fills the window in $8{,}192 / 100 \approx$ **82 seconds**; Moshi's layout, one temporal position per 80 ms frame, covers $8{,}192 / 12.5 \approx$ **655 seconds**. How the codebooks are arranged changes the sequence length by a factor of eight, so symbol counts alone do not give compute or KV-cache cost: those depend on the architecture (here, a large temporal transformer over 12.5 positions per second plus a small depth transformer per frame). High-resolution images and long audio still strain context in ways text rarely does, which is why multimodal systems compress aggressively.

## What changes: the objective

Next-token prediction is not the only objective, and for some data it is not the best:

- **Contrastive learning** trains two encoders so that matching pairs end up close. CLIP learned image representations from 400 million image–caption pairs by predicting which caption goes with which image, and could then classify images zero-shot by comparing them with text descriptions of classes ([Radford et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020)). The supervision is the pairing, not the next token.
- **Denoising** (diffusion and flow matching) learns to turn noise into data step by step, and dominates image, video and much audio generation; the [Foundations post on diffusion and flow](/blog/foundations-diffusion-flow/) explains it.
- **Encoder–decoder transcription** maps one modality to text: [Whisper](/paper-reviews/whisper/) is trained on 680,000 hours of audio paired with web transcripts, with next-token prediction on the text side only.
- **Next-token prediction over codec tokens** generates audio the way a language model generates text, as in speech language models.

The choice follows the data: is there a natural order to predict, paired data to align, or a continuous signal to reconstruct?

## Connecting modalities to a language model

A common way to build a vision–language model reuses components: a pre-trained **vision encoder**, a pre-trained **language model**, and a small learned **projection** that maps image features into the language model's embedding space, so image patches enter as if they were tokens. LLaVA built this way and instruction-tuned it on image–text conversations generated with GPT-4 from image captions ([Liu et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08485)). The training recipe then looks familiar: align the projection first, then fine-tune on instructions, as in the [SFT post](/blog/model-development-supervised-fine-tuning/), with the same questions about data mixtures, masking and evaluation.

## What changes: judging quality

Evaluation is where modalities differ most. Text has exact-match and judged quality; images are judged by human preference and by distributional measures; speech by intelligibility (word error rate of a transcription), similarity to a target speaker, and listening tests. The [speech evaluation post](/blog/speech-evaluation-metrics/) walks through those metrics. The discipline from the [application-evaluation post](/blog/model-development-evaluating-applications/) holds unchanged: real test sets, paired comparisons, error bars and suspicion of any single number.

Everyday picture: a factory that once made only books retooling for photographs and records. The presses, the logistics, the accounting and the quality control are reused; what changes is how each product is cut into pieces, how big each order is, and how a customer decides whether it is good.


## What to remember

- Transformers, training mechanics, distributed training, data discipline, scaling laws and post-training transfer across modalities.
- The first design question for a new modality is tokenization: patches for images (196 for a 224 × 224 image), codec frames for speech (Mimi: 12.5 frames per second, 100 codec symbols per second over 8 codebooks).
- Sequence length depends on layout as well as on the codec: Moshi models 12.5 positions per second and generates each frame's 8 codebooks with a depth transformer, so symbol counts are not a direct compute or KV-cache multiplier.
- Objectives follow the data: next-token prediction, contrastive alignment of pairs (CLIP), denoising (diffusion, flow) or transcription (Whisper).
- Vision–language models often join a vision encoder to a language model through a learned projection, then instruction-tune.
- Quality is judged differently per modality; the evaluation discipline is the same.
