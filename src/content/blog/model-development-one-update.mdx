**Question: which parts of one training update change what a language model learns?**

Four conventions: which positions are **targets**, what each position may **attend to**, how per-token losses are **averaged**, and how the optimizer turns gradients into a **step**. Get any of them wrong and training still runs; it just optimizes something else.

The worked batch below carries the details. Everything later in this series, from corpus building to distributed training, has to feed this update or run it faster without changing its meaning.

We follow one small batch through all four. If backpropagation, cross-entropy or Adam are new, the Foundations posts on [backpropagation](/blog/foundations-neurons-backprop/), [softmax and cross-entropy](/blog/foundations-loss-gradient-descent/), [optimisers](/blog/foundations-optimisers/) and [language models](/blog/foundations-language-models/) cover them. The [small training run](/blog/model-development-small-training-run/) that follows puts exactly this update into a loop.

## Targets: the inputs shifted by one

For `<bos> the cat sat <eos>`, each position predicts the next token:

| Position | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sees up to | `<bos>` | `the` | `cat` | `sat` | `<eos>` |
| Should predict | `the` | `cat` | `sat` | `<eos>` | nothing |

Five tokens give four targets. In code the targets usually start as a copy of the inputs, `labels`, and the shift happens inside the loss. In Hugging Face Transformers 4.51.3, `ForCausalLMLoss` appends one ignored label and drops the first, so position $t$ is scored against the token at $t + 1$, and any label of `-100` is ignored. nanoGPT shifts in the data loader instead and ignores `-1`. Either works; mixing the two conventions shifts twice.

An ignored label removes that position's loss term, so its prediction sends **no direct gradient**. It does not freeze the token: its input still gets gradients through later positions that attend to it. This is what happens to prompt tokens when fine-tuning masks their labels (a two-line example is in the [optional section](#optional-the-backward-pass-by-hand)).

## One batch: packing, padding and document boundaries

Three short documents fill two rows of six positions:

- document A: `<bos> the cat <eos>`
- document B: `<bos> cat`, cut off at the end of the row
- document C: `<bos> cat sat <eos>`

Row 0 **packs** A and B together so no slot is wasted; row 1 holds C and is **padded** with two `<pad>` tokens. Three kinds of position get no target:

- the **last position** of each row, which has no next token;
- positions whose next token is **padding**;
- A's last token, whose next token is B's `<bos>`. Predicting the start of an unrelated document teaches the packing format, not language, so many pipelines ignore this **boundary** target. Others keep it; know which you do.

That leaves 4 targets in row 0 and 3 in row 1: **seven targets out of twelve positions**.

## Attention masks: what each position may see

Labels say what a position must predict; the attention mask says what it may **look at**. They are separate decisions. In a decoder, position $t$ may attend to position $s$ only if $s \le t$, $s$ is not padding, and, in a packed row, $s$ belongs to the same document.

The third rule is the one people skip. In row 0, position 4 is B's `<bos>` and must predict `cat`. Give the five tokens it could see the illustrative values 1, 2, 3, 4 and 1, and let attention simply average what is allowed:

- **causal mask only:** $(1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 1)/5 = 2.2$;
- **with the document mask:** it sees only itself, $1/1 = 1$.

Same token, same target, different input: without the document mask, B's prediction depends on unrelated document A. Llama 3 masks attention between documents and reports that this had limited impact in standard pre-training but mattered for continued pre-training on very long sequences ([Llama 3, §3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3)), so measure it on your data rather than assume.

Two details that look like the same decision are not. Padding at the **end** of a row never reaches a real position under the causal rule; the padding rule matters for **left** padding, used when generating from prompts of different lengths. And **position IDs** are a third choice: with rotary embeddings (RoPE), attention depends only on the distance between positions, so isolating documents is the mask's job and does not require restarting position IDs. Codebases differ here; check yours.

## The loss: one position, then the token mean

At one position the model gives a logit $z_v$ for every vocabulary token $v$, and the loss is minus the log of the probability of the target $y$:

$$
\ell = -\log \frac{e^{z_y}}{\sum_v e^{z_v}} = \log\sum_v e^{z_v} \;-\; z_y .
$$

With four tokens and equal logits, every token has probability $1/4$ and the loss is $\ln 4 \approx 1.386$ whatever the target: a model that knows nothing scores $\ln V$ for vocabulary size $V$. (Implementations subtract the largest logit before the log-sum-exp; it changes nothing mathematically and prevents overflow.)

The batch has seven such losses, and the update needs one number. Two natural averages give **different objectives**. With made-up losses, row A has three targets at loss 1 and row B one target at loss 3:

- **token mean:** $(1 + 1 + 1 + 3)/4 = 1.5$, every token weighted $1/4$;
- **mean of row means:** $\left(\frac{1+1+1}{3} + \frac{3}{1}\right)/2 = 2$, so B's single token weighs $\frac12$ and each of A's $\frac16$.

Pre-training normally intends the token mean, so every token of text matters equally:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{(b,t)\,\in\,\mathcal{T}} \log p_\theta\!\left(x_{b,t+1} \mid \text{what position } (b,t) \text{ may see}\right),
$$

where $\mathcal{T}$ is the set of target positions (seven in our batch), $N = |\mathcal{T}|$, and $p_\theta$ is computed under the attention mask. `ForCausalLMLoss` uses the token mean by default.

## Gradient accumulation must keep the token mean

When a batch does not fit in memory, the step is split into **microbatches** whose gradients are added before one optimizer step. The natural code averages each microbatch and divides by the number of microbatches. With the two rows above as two microbatches, that gives $\frac12(1 + 3) = 2$: the mean of row means, not the token mean.

The fix is to divide each microbatch's **summed** loss by the step's total target count $N = 4$: $\frac{3}{4} + \frac{3}{4} = 1.5$. Gradients add linearly, so the accumulated gradient then equals the token-mean gradient exactly. (Backpropagating the plain sums and dividing the accumulated gradient by $N$ once, before clipping, is the same objective; the two need not be bitwise identical because floating-point additions happen in a different order.)

The error grows with the imbalance. If microbatches hold 2 and 8 valid tokens, the token mean weights their mean losses 20% and 80%; mean-then-divide weights them 50% and 50%, so each token in the small microbatch counts four times as much as one in the large one. With 1,000 and 10 tokens the factor is 100. Packing rows to a fixed length makes counts similar and hides the problem; variable-length batches expose it.

Two edge cases need explicit handling:

- **A step with no targets** has $N = 0$. Skip the optimizer step and record it rather than divide by zero; with several GPUs, $N$ is the count over all of them and all must make the same decision.
- **Where $N$ comes from in a framework.** The Transformers 4.51.3 Trainer counts `labels.ne(-100)` on the **unshifted** labels, while the loss drops each row's first label when shifting, so for short rows the count is slightly too large and varies from step to step. Details are in the [optional section](#how-the-transformers-trainer-counts-targets); check the rule in the version you use.

## Clipping: once per step, and not a repair

Most pre-training recipes cap the **global norm** of the gradient, treating every entry of every parameter as one vector: $g \leftarrow g \cdot \min\left(1,\ c/(\|g\| + 10^{-6})\right)$ for cap $c$. Gradients $(3, 4)$ have norm 5; with a cap of 1 they become $(0.6, 0.8)$, the same direction with norm 1. PyTorch 2.6's `clip_grad_norm_` computes exactly this. Because the norm is global, one huge gradient shrinks every other parameter's too.

- **Clip once per step**, after accumulation and before the optimizer step. With FP16 loss scaling, unscale first so the cap applies to the true gradient.
- **Clipping does not repair non-finite gradients.** In PyTorch 2.6, one NaN entry makes the norm NaN and every gradient NaN; one Inf makes the norm Inf, zeroing the finite entries and leaving NaN where the Inf was. No error is raised by default. Check that the returned norm is finite and skip the step if not.
- **A clipped gradient is not a proportionally smaller update**, because Adam divides by its own running estimate of gradient size, as the next example shows.

## One AdamW step

Adam keeps running averages $m$ of the gradient and $v$ of its square, both starting at zero, and steps by $\gamma\,\hat m/(\sqrt{\hat v}+\epsilon)$ after bias correction:

$$
m \leftarrow \beta_1 m + (1-\beta_1)\,g, \quad v \leftarrow \beta_2 v + (1-\beta_2)\,g^2, \quad
\hat m = \frac{m}{1-\beta_1^{\,k}}, \quad \hat v = \frac{v}{1-\beta_2^{\,k}}, \quad
\theta \leftarrow \theta - \gamma\,\frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat v} + \epsilon},
$$

with learning rate $\gamma$, decay rates $\beta_1, \beta_2$, a small $\epsilon$ and step number $k$ from 1.

Take $\theta = 1$, $\gamma = 0.1$, $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.99$, ignore $\epsilon$, and a first gradient $g = 0.2$: $m = 0.02$, $v = 0.0004$, $\hat m = 0.2$, $\hat v = 0.04$, and the step is $0.1 \times 0.2/0.2 = 0.1$. With a gradient a hundred times smaller, $g = 0.002$, the step is again $0.1$. **On the first step every parameter with a nonzero gradient moves by the learning rate, in the direction of its sign**, whatever the gradient's size, and whether or not it was clipped.

History matters after that. Scaling the **whole** gradient history by one positive factor leaves the step unchanged, but rescaling **one** step does not: gradients $(0.2, 0.2)$ give a second step of $0.1$, while $(0.2, 0.1)$ give about $0.093$. That is why a step-to-step change in the loss normalizer is not harmless just because Adam is called "scale invariant".

**AdamW** also shrinks each decayed weight towards zero, separately from the gradient step. PyTorch 2.6 multiplies the weight by $1 - \gamma\lambda$ first, where $\lambda$ is `weight_decay`; with $\lambda = 0.1$ the example becomes $1 \times 0.99 - 0.1 = 0.89$. The original paper scales decay differently, by a factor of the learning rate, which matters when you copy a recipe between codebases ([optional section](#two-weight-decay-conventions)). Which parameters decay is a separate choice; nanoGPT decays matrices and embeddings, not biases or normalization weights.

## The same step in code

A minimal step for a Hugging Face causal language model that keeps the token mean under accumulation and skips bad steps:

```python
num_targets = sum((mb["labels"][:, 1:] != -100).sum() for mb in microbatches)
if num_targets == 0:
    skip_step()                       # nothing to learn from; never divide by zero
else:
    for mb in microbatches:
        logits = model(input_ids=mb["input_ids"],
                       attention_mask=mb["attention_mask"]).logits
        loss_sum = F.cross_entropy(
            logits[:, :-1].reshape(-1, logits.size(-1)).float(),  # predict t+1 from t
            mb["labels"][:, 1:].reshape(-1),
            ignore_index=-100, reduction="sum")
        (loss_sum / num_targets).backward()        # gradients add up to the token mean
    norm = torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), max_norm=1.0)
    if torch.isfinite(norm):
        optimizer.step()
    scheduler.step()
    optimizer.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
```

Slicing `logits[:, :-1]` against `labels[:, 1:]` performs the shift, so do not also pass pre-shifted labels. In Hugging Face, `attention_mask` marks padding, not document boundaries; isolating packed documents needs a separate mechanism, such as variable-length attention kernels driven by document lengths. At realistic sizes the loss itself costs memory: `ForCausalLMLoss` upcasts logits to FP32, $4096 \times 32000 \times 4 \approx 524$ MB per row for a 32,000-token vocabulary, which is why large codebases compute the loss in chunks.

## What goes wrong, and the check that catches it

| Mistake | Symptom | Check before training |
|---|---|---|
| Shifting twice, or not at all | Loss unusually high, or near zero within a few hundred steps (copying the input) | Decode one row's inputs and targets side by side |
| Padding or boundaries not ignored | Training loss too low; the model learns `<pad>` | Count targets per batch: padding must contribute none |
| Mean-then-divide accumulation | Loss changes when the microbatch size changes at a fixed batch | Normalize by the step's total target count |
| Non-finite gradients clipped silently | Weights and loss become NaN | Check the clip norm is finite; skip the step if not |
| Weight-decay convention mixed up | A copied recipe does not reproduce | Compute the per-step shrink explicitly |

The first check is the cheapest and catches the most: before any long run, print one row of inputs, the targets they are scored against and the mask, and read them.

## Try it yourself

- **Two averages.** Row A has losses $[2, 2]$ and row B $[5]$. Token mean: $9/3 =$ **3**; mean of row means: $(2 + 5)/2 =$ **3.5**. As two microbatches, dividing each sum by the step's 3 targets gives $4/3 + 5/3 =$ **3**.
- **Unequal microbatches.** Microbatches with 3 and 12 valid tokens: under the token mean their mean losses get weights **20% and 80%**.
- **Clipping.** Gradients $(6, 8)$ with a cap of 2: the norm is 10, so the result is **(1.2, 1.6)**.
- **First Adam step.** Learning rate 0.1, gradient $-5$: the parameter **increases by 0.1**, and by 0.1 again for gradient $-0.0005$.

## Optional: the backward pass by hand

**Ignored positions still learn.** If position 1 averages the inputs of positions 0 and 1, $h_1 = (x_0 + x_1)/2$, with loss $\ell_1 = h_1^2$, then for $x_0 = 1$, $x_1 = 3$: $h_1 = 2$ and $\partial \ell_1/\partial x_0 = 2h_1 \cdot \frac12 = 2$. Position 0 has no target, yet its input moves.

**The gradient at the logits.** With three tokens, logits $(\ln 2, 0, 0)$ give probabilities $p = \left(\frac12, \frac14, \frac14\right)$; for target $e_y = (1, 0, 0)$, the loss is $\ln 2$ and

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z} = p - e_y = \left(-\tfrac12,\ \tfrac14,\ \tfrac14\right):
$$

raise the target's logit, lower each wrong token's in proportion to its probability. Under the token mean with $N = 4$, this position contributes $\left(-\frac18, \frac1{16}, \frac1{16}\right)$. If the logits come from a hidden vector $h = (1, 2)$ through an output matrix $W$, $z = hW$, the weight gradient is the outer product

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W} = h^{\top}\,\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z}
= \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}\left(-\tfrac18,\ \tfrac1{16},\ \tfrac1{16}\right)
= \begin{pmatrix} -\tfrac18 & \tfrac1{16} & \tfrac1{16} \\[0.4ex] -\tfrac14 & \tfrac18 & \tfrac18 \end{pmatrix},
$$

summed over all target positions in the batch. In a Transformer the same $p - e_y$ signal enters the output layer and flows back through every block.

### How the Transformers Trainer counts targets

In Transformers 4.51.3 the Trainer counts targets when the model accepts loss keyword arguments (as `LlamaForCausalLM` does) or when you pass your own loss function. It fetches all of a step's microbatches, counts `labels.ne(-100).sum()` and passes it as `num_items_in_batch`, and the loss divides its sum by that. The count uses the **unshifted** labels while the loss drops each row's first label, so two rows of six tokens starting with `<bos>` count 10 targets although 8 are predicted. The ratio depends on the number of rows and targets in each step, so it slightly reweights steps against each other: negligible for pre-training rows of thousands of tokens, larger for short fine-tuning sequences.

### Two weight-decay conventions

[Loshchilov and Hutter (2019), Algorithm 2](https://arxiv.org/html/1711.05101v3) update $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta_t\left(\alpha\,\hat m/(\sqrt{\hat v}+\epsilon) + \lambda\theta\right)$, with base learning rate $\alpha$ and schedule multiplier $\eta_t$: decay is multiplied by the schedule but **not** by the learning rate. PyTorch's learning rate at step $t$ is $\gamma_t = \alpha\,\eta_t$, so its shrink per step is $\alpha\,\eta_t\,\lambda_{\text{PyTorch}}$ against the paper's $\eta_t\,\lambda_{\text{paper}}$. They match when

$$
\lambda_{\text{paper}} = \alpha\,\lambda_{\text{PyTorch}} .
$$

A PyTorch recipe of learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$ and weight decay 0.1 corresponds to $\lambda_{\text{paper}} = 3\times10^{-5}$.
