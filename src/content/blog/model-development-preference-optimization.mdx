**Question: how does "response A is better than response B" become a gradient, and when would you use DPO instead of a reward model?**

A **reward model** fits a score to each response so that the probability of preferring A over B is a sigmoid of their score difference (the Bradley–Terry model); the policy is then trained to raise that score while a KL penalty keeps it close to a reference model. **DPO** skips the separate reward model: the same KL-regularized objective has a closed-form optimum, so the policy can be trained directly on preference pairs, raising the preferred response's probability relative to the reference and lowering the rejected one's, most strongly when the implicit reward orders the pair wrongly. Use DPO when you have a fixed set of pairs and want a simple, stable offline method; use a reward model when you need to score new samples, as online RL does.

After [SFT](/blog/model-development-supervised-fine-tuning/) a model answers in the right format, but it only imitates. Many qualities we want, such as helpfulness, tone and honesty about uncertainty, are hard to demonstrate and easy to judge. This post needs only the SFT post and basic probability.

## From comparisons to a reward

The data is a set of triples: a prompt $x$, a preferred response $y_w$ and a rejected one $y_l$. InstructGPT showed labelers 4 to 9 responses at a time and had them rank them; each ranking yields many pairs ([Ouyang et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155), §3.5). Judgements are noisy: InstructGPT's trained labelers agreed with each other 72.6% of the time (§3.4). That is not a ceiling on a model's agreement with an individual labeler. If two labelers each pick the truly better answer 80% of the time, independently, they agree $0.8^2 + 0.2^2 = 68\%$ of the time, while a model that always knows the better answer agrees with either one 80% of the time. Noise does limit how well we can *measure* a reward model, and it blurs the training signal.

The standard way to turn pairs into a number is the **Bradley–Terry model**: each response has a hidden score, a **reward** $r(x, y)$, and

$$
P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\big(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)\big), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}.
$$

Only the difference of rewards matters. A difference of 0 gives a coin flip; a difference of 1 gives $\sigma(1) \approx 0.73$; a difference of 3 gives 0.95. Read backwards: if response A is preferred to B in 3 of 4 comparisons, the best-fitting reward gap is $\ln 3 \approx 1.10$.

A **reward model** is a language model with its output layer replaced by a single number, trained on the pairs by maximizing that probability, that is, minimizing $-\log \sigma(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l))$ (InstructGPT Eq. 1; [DPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290) Eq. 2). It is logistic regression on the difference of two scores.

## The objective: more reward, but stay close

Given a reward model, the obvious move is to make the policy produce high-reward responses. Pushed too far, that fails: the reward model is only accurate near the kinds of responses it was trained on, and a policy that wanders far from them finds responses the reward model scores highly for the wrong reasons. So the standard objective adds a penalty for moving away from a **reference policy** $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, usually the SFT model:

$$
\max_{\pi}\ \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi(\cdot \mid x)}\big[r(x, y)\big] - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi(\cdot \mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)\big).
$$

- $\pi$: the policy being trained, the model's distribution over responses.
- $\mathrm{KL}$: how far $\pi$ has moved from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.
- $\beta$: the exchange rate between reward and staying close.

This objective has an exact solution ([DPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290), §4, Eq. 4):

$$
\pi^*(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\,\exp\!\big(r(x, y)/\beta\big),
$$

where $Z(x)$ is whatever makes the probabilities sum to 1. The optimal policy reweights the reference policy by exponentiated reward.

Work it on three possible responses with reference probabilities $(0.5, 0.3, 0.2)$ and rewards $(0, 1, 2)$:

- With $\beta = 1$: weights $0.5$, $0.3e \approx 0.82$, $0.2e^2 \approx 1.48$; normalized, **$(0.18, 0.29, 0.53)$**. The best response becomes the most likely, but the others keep some probability.
- With $\beta = 0.5$, reward counts double: $(0.04, 0.16, 0.80)$.
- With $\beta = 2$: $(0.33, 0.32, 0.35)$; and as $\beta$ grows, the policy returns to the reference.

$\beta$ is a dial between "trust the reward model" and "stay where you were".

## DPO: optimizing preferences directly

The usual way to reach $\pi^*$ is reinforcement learning against the reward model, the subject of the [next post](/blog/model-development-reinforcement-learning/). **Direct Preference Optimization** (DPO) takes a shortcut ([Rafailov et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290), §4). Rearranging the optimal policy gives the reward in terms of the policy:

$$
r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
$$

In the toy example, $\log(\pi^*/\pi_{\text{ref}})$ is $(-1.03, -0.03, 0.97)$: the rewards $(0, 1, 2)$ shifted by a constant. Substituting this into Bradley–Terry, the constant $\beta \log Z(x)$ appears in both rewards and cancels in their difference. What remains is a loss on the policy alone (Eq. 7):

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}\right).
$$

Each $\log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)$ is the sum of the log-probabilities of the response's tokens under the model, the same quantity as the SFT loss with the sign flipped. So DPO needs no reward model and no sampling during training: for each pair, run the policy and the frozen reference on both responses and compute one logistic loss. The quantity $\beta \log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})$ acts as an **implicit reward**.

The gradient shows what DPO does. On the three-response toy with $\beta = 0.1$, preferring response 3 over response 1, the first gradient on the logits is $(+0.05, 0, -0.05)$: push the preferred response up and the rejected one down, by $\beta\,\sigma(-\text{margin})$. The weight $\sigma(-\text{margin})$ is large when the implicit reward orders the pair wrongly and small when it already orders it right, so well-learned pairs stop contributing. After four steps the probabilities move from $(0.5, 0.3, 0.2)$ to $(0.43, 0.32, 0.26)$, and the response not in the pair moved too, through the softmax.

**Try it: compute a DPO loss.** At the start of training $\pi_\theta = \pi_{\text{ref}}$, so both log-ratios are 0. What is the loss? Later, the chosen response's log-ratio is +0.2 and the rejected one's −0.1, with $\beta = 0.1$. And if they are −0.5 and −1.5?

**Answer.** At the start the margin is 0 and the loss is $-\log \sigma(0) = \ln 2 \approx$ **0.693**. Then the margin is $0.1 \times (0.2 - (-0.1)) = 0.03$ and the loss **0.678**. In the third case the margin is $0.1 \times (-0.5 + 1.5) = 0.1$ and the loss **0.644**, lower still, although *both* responses became less likely than under the reference. DPO only cares about the difference, so it can lower the probability of the chosen response as long as the rejected one falls faster. This is a known behaviour of DPO and one reason practitioners monitor the chosen response's log-probability during training.

## What practice has found

- **Off-policy data has limits.** DPO learns from fixed pairs, often generated by other models. Tülu 3 found that adding **on-policy** pairs, with responses sampled from the model being trained, improved results, as did increasing the number of unique prompts ([Tülu 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15124), §5.3). Llama 3 likewise trained each round's DPO mainly on preference data collected with its latest models, so the data matched the policy being optimized ([Llama 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783), §4.1.4).
- **Length is a confound.** Summed log-probabilities grow with length, and judges, human or model, tend to prefer longer answers. Tülu 3 compared standard DPO, SimPO and length-normalized DPO, which divides each log-ratio by the response length, and found that only the length-normalized variant beat its starting checkpoint on average (§5.4.1, Table 18). Its final 8B setting used $\beta = 5$ with a learning rate of $5 \times 10^{-7}$ (Table 20); $\beta$'s scale changes with normalization, so values do not transfer between variants.
- **Formatting tokens can distort the loss.** Llama 3 masked special formatting tokens out of the DPO loss, because tokens shared by both responses are pushed up and down at once, and added a negative-log-likelihood term with coefficient 0.2 on the chosen response to keep its probability from falling (§4.1.4).
- **DPO or reinforcement learning?** Llama 3 explored PPO but found DPO needed less compute at large scale and performed better on instruction following (§4.1.4). Tülu 3 used DPO for preferences and added reinforcement learning only where answers can be verified. The next post covers when online RL earns its cost.
