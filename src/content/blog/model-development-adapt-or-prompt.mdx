**Question: the model fails on your task. Should you change the prompt, add retrieval or fine-tune?**

Diagnose the failure first. If the model does not know **what you want** (wrong format, tone or constraint), it is an instruction problem: prompt. If it lacks **information** (your documents, recent facts), it is a knowledge problem: retrieve, which keeps knowledge current and citable. If it has both but does not **do it reliably or cheaply enough**, it is a behaviour problem: fine-tune on a few hundred to a few thousand good examples of the behaviour. When you fine-tune, **LoRA** trains a low-rank update instead of the full weights: rank 8 on Llama 3 8B's query and value projections is 3.4 million trainable parameters, 0.04% of the model, and can be merged into the weights with no serving cost.

The previous posts gave the three tools: [prompting](/blog/model-development-prompting-context/), [retrieval and tools](/blog/model-development-retrieval-tools/) and, from the post-training track, [fine-tuning](/blog/model-development-supervised-fine-tuning/). They are often presented as rungs of a ladder, but they fix different problems.

## Three problems, three tools

Look at your evaluation set's failures and ask what is missing:

- **The model does not know what you want.** It answers a different question, in the wrong format or tone, or ignores a constraint. That is an **instruction** problem: fix it with clearer instructions and examples.
- **The model lacks information.** It does not know your products, last week's prices or the contents of a contract. That is a **knowledge** problem: fix it with **retrieval**, which supplies the information at question time and keeps it current. Fine-tuning is a poor way to add facts: it is slow to update, hard to verify, and a fine-tuned model may still recall them unreliably and without citations.
- **The model knows what to do and has the information, but does not do it well or reliably enough.** It cannot hold a specialized format across thousands of requests, a smaller model needs to match a larger one's behaviour, or the prompt needed to get the behaviour is too long and costly. That is a **behaviour** problem: fix it with **fine-tuning** on examples of the behaviour.

These combine. A support assistant may use retrieval for policy documents, a fine-tuned small model for the company's response style, and a prompt for the current case.

**Try it: choose a method.** Which would you start with for each case?
1. A legal assistant must answer questions about a client's 400-page contract.
2. A classifier must label support tickets into 30 categories; a large model with a long prompt of examples does it well but is too slow and expensive at 2 million tickets a day.
3. A chatbot answers correctly but too casually for a bank.

**Answer.** (1) **Retrieval**: the knowledge is private, specific and long; retrieve relevant clauses and cite them. (2) **Fine-tuning** a small model on the large model's labels (distillation, from the [post-training data post](/blog/model-development-post-training-data/#responses-who-writes-the-answer)): the behaviour exists, the cost is the problem. (3) **Prompting** first: tone is an instruction problem; fine-tune only if instructions and examples cannot make it consistent.

## What fine-tuning needs

Fine-tuning replaces a long prompt with a change to the weights, which removes the prompt's cost at every request. It needs:

- **Examples of the target behaviour**, typically hundreds to a few thousand of high quality for a narrow task; more for broad behaviour. Their quality and coverage matter more than their number, as the [SFT post](/blog/model-development-supervised-fine-tuning/#what-the-data-teaches) showed for Tülu 3.
- **An evaluation set** separate from the training examples, including cases the fine-tuning should *not* change, because fine-tuning on a narrow task can degrade others.
- **Open weights,** or a provider's fine-tuning service.
- **Compute and memory.** Full fine-tuning of an 8-billion-parameter model with AdamW needs about 128 GB for weights, gradients and optimizer state alone, as the [GPU post](/blog/model-development-gpu-memory-compute/#what-training-keeps-in-memory) worked out. That is where LoRA comes in.

## LoRA: training a low-rank change

**Low-Rank Adaptation** (LoRA) freezes the pre-trained weights and learns only a small change to some of them ([Hu et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09685), §4.1). For a weight matrix $W_0$ of shape $d \times k$, the adapted layer computes

$$
h = W_0 x + \frac{\alpha}{r} B A x, \qquad B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r},\ A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k},\ r \ll \min(d, k).
$$

- $A$ and $B$ are the only trained parameters; $BA$ is a $d \times k$ matrix of rank at most $r$.
- $\alpha/r$ scales the update, so changing $r$ does not require retuning the learning rate.
- $A$ starts random and $B$ starts at **zero**, so $BA = 0$ and training begins from exactly the pre-trained model.

The saving is large. A $4{,}096 \times 4{,}096$ matrix has 16.8 million weights; a rank-8 adapter for it has $8 \times 4{,}096 + 4{,}096 \times 8 = 65{,}536$, 0.39%. Applying rank 8 to the query and value projections of all 32 layers of Llama 3 8B trains 3.4 million parameters, 0.04% of the model. Only those need gradients and optimizer state; the frozen 8B weights need just their BF16 copy, 16 GB.

Why should a low-rank change be enough? The paper's hypothesis is that the change fine-tuning needs has low "intrinsic rank", and its experiments support it for the tasks studied: on GPT-3 175B, LoRA matched or exceeded full fine-tuning on three benchmarks (Table 4), a rank as small as one sufficed when adapting the query and value matrices (§7.2, Table 6), and training memory fell from 1.2 TB to 350 GB (§4.2). The same paper notes that on GPT-2 Medium performance peaked at larger ranks, so the right rank is task- and model-dependent. A toy shows the mechanism: when the true change to a 16 × 16 layer has rank 2, a rank-2 LoRA trained on its outputs recovers it to a loss of $3 \times 10^{-6}$.

**No cost at inference.** After training, $\frac{\alpha}{r}BA$ can be added into $W_0$ once. The merged layer is an ordinary matrix of the original shape, so the model runs exactly as fast as before; on the toy, the merged layer's outputs equal base plus adapter. Alternatively the adapters can stay separate: many small adapters (tens of megabytes each) can share one base model, swapped per customer or task.

**QLoRA** goes further by storing the frozen base model in 4 bits, about 4 GB for 8 billion parameters, while training LoRA adapters through it. Its authors fine-tuned a 65-billion-parameter model on a single 48 GB GPU while preserving 16-bit fine-tuning quality ([Dettmers et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14314), abstract). Quantization is covered in the [inference post](/blog/model-development-inference-serving/#quantization).

LoRA's limits follow from its design: a low-rank update may be too small for large changes in knowledge or capability, the choice of which matrices to adapt and the rank matter, and LoRA results are usually reported against full fine-tuning on specific tasks, not in general. The [LoRA review](/paper-reviews/lora/) looks at that evidence.
