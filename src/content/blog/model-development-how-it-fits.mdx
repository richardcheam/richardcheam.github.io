The series took decisions one stage at a time: [data](/blog/model-development-pretraining-corpus/), [tokenization](/blog/model-development-tokenization/), [architecture](/blog/model-development-decoder-architecture/), [scale](/blog/model-development-scaling-laws/), [distributed training](/blog/model-development-running-at-scale/), [post-training](/blog/model-development-supervised-fine-tuning/), [serving](/blog/model-development-inference-serving/) and [application evaluation](/blog/model-development-evaluating-applications/). In a real project they are not independent. A choice made early fixes costs that appear much later, and a requirement discovered late can only be met cheaply if it was anticipated early. This short post traces the connections.

## The main couplings

| Decision | What it constrains later |
|---|---|
| **Tokenizer** | Compute and context per language; vocabulary parameters; how much of the corpus a token budget covers; serving cost per request |
| **Data mixture** | Which capabilities the base model has; what post-training can draw out; what evaluation will find |
| **Model size and token budget** | Training cost, but also serving latency and memory for the model's whole life |
| **Attention heads (GQA)** | KV-cache size, and therefore batch size, throughput and cost when serving |
| **Context length** | Training memory and cost; position-encoding choices; the usefulness of retrieval |
| **Post-training recipe** | Format, refusal and reasoning behaviour that applications rely on; regressions applications must test |
| **Evaluation set** | What every earlier decision is optimized towards |

## Tracing a requirement: serving Khmer users cheaply

Suppose the requirement is a Khmer-language assistant that answers in under two seconds at low cost.

1. **Tokenizer.** Under an English-centred tokenizer, Khmer cost 15 times as many tokens as English for the same content; under a multilingual one, 6.5 times ([tokenization post](/blog/model-development-tokenization/#what-the-same-text-costs)). Every later cost is multiplied by that factor, so a tokenizer with Khmer pieces is the single largest saving available, and it can only be chosen before pre-training.
2. **Data.** Khmer is a low-resource language. Its share of the mixture, and how many times it is repeated, decide how well the model learns it ([mixtures post](/blog/model-development-data-mixtures/#three-ways-to-set-weights)). Language identification must not discard mixed Khmer–English pages ([corpus post](/blog/model-development-pretraining-corpus/#keeping-one-language)).
3. **Size.** A model that serves many requests should be smaller and trained longer than the compute-optimal size ([scaling laws](/blog/model-development-scaling-laws/#when-the-model-has-to-be-served)), but a small model has less capacity for a language it saw little of. This tension has to be measured, not assumed.
4. **Architecture and serving.** Long Khmer token sequences fill the KV cache sooner, so grouped-query attention and quantization matter more than for English ([inference post](/blog/model-development-inference-serving/#the-kv-cache-decides-how-many-users-fit)).
5. **Evaluation.** The test set must be in Khmer, with Khmer-speaking judges or validated automatic metrics; English benchmarks say little about the requirement.

A team that fixes the tokenizer and mixture first and discovers the Khmer requirement at step 5 can only patch it with fine-tuning and retrieval, at higher serving cost for the life of the product.

## Tracing a requirement: reliable mathematical answers

Now the requirement is that answers to arithmetic and algebra problems are right, not just plausible.

1. **Data and base model.** Mathematical skill comes mostly from pre-training data; DeepSeekMath's 120-billion-token math corpus did much of the work before any RL ([DeepSeekMath review](/paper-reviews/deepseekmath-grpo/)). The same SFT mix gave 31.5 on MATH from one base model and 56.4 from a math-specialized one ([SFT post](/blog/model-development-supervised-fine-tuning/#what-the-data-teaches)).
2. **Post-training.** Answers are checkable, so verifiable rewards and RL fit ([RL post](/blog/model-development-reinforcement-learning/#verifiable-rewards)), with prompts chosen at the edge of the model's ability so groups carry signal.
3. **Inference.** Sampling several solutions and voting improves accuracy ([prompting post](/blog/model-development-prompting-context/#sampling-several-times-and-voting)), but multiplies serving cost; a calculator tool makes arithmetic exact for the price of a tool call ([retrieval and tools](/blog/model-development-retrieval-tools/#tools)).
4. **Evaluation.** Pass@1 for users, Pass@K and Maj@K to tell sharpening from new capability, and fresh problems the training data could not contain.

Here the cheapest reliable system may combine a moderately sized model, RL with a verifier, and a tool, rather than a much larger model.

## A real example: Llama 3's interlocking choices

Llama 3's report is useful precisely because its decisions visibly anticipate later stages ([Llama 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783), §3):

- a **128,000-token vocabulary**, adding 28,000 tokens for non-English languages, improved English compression from 3.17 to 3.94 characters per token, letting the model read more text for the same training compute (§3.2);
- **grouped-query attention with 8 key-value heads** to reduce the KV cache and speed up decoding (§3.2);
- a **flagship size chosen from scaling laws** fitted on its own data mix, with the flat optimum used to pick a convenient 405B (§3.2.1), while smaller models were trained far longer for cheaper serving;
- **annealing on high-quality data**, which helped the 8B model more than the 405B, and a document mask that mattered for long-context training (§3.1.3, §3.2).

None of these is remarkable alone. Together they show the habit this series has tried to teach: every choice is judged by what it costs or enables at the other stages.

**Try it: trace a requirement.** Your application must cite the document behind every answer, from a collection that changes daily. Which stages does that requirement reach?

**Answer.** It is mostly a **retrieval** requirement: knowledge that changes daily belongs in an index, not in weights, and citations need retrieved passages ([adaptation post](/blog/model-development-adapt-or-prompt/#three-problems-three-tools)). It reaches **context length and serving** (passages consume tokens and KV cache), **prompting or fine-tuning** for a consistent citation format, **evaluation** (does the cited passage actually support the claim?), and **security** (retrieved documents can carry injected instructions). It barely touches pre-training: a model that follows instructions and handles long contexts is enough.


## What to remember

- Stages are coupled: the tokenizer, mixture, size and attention design fix costs that appear at serving time.
- Start from the requirement hardest to meet in production, and trace it back through evaluation, serving, post-training, architecture, data and tokenizer before fixing any stage.
- Some requirements can only be met cheaply early (tokenizer, language coverage); others belong late (fresh knowledge in retrieval).
- Read technical reports for how decisions interlock, not only for their final numbers.
