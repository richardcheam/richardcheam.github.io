**Question: when should you split the model itself across GPUs, and which split goes where?**

Split the model when data parallelism and sharding are not enough: a layer's matrix multiplications are too slow at the batch you need, a sequence's activations do not fit, or a mixture-of-experts model has more experts than one GPU holds. **Tensor parallelism** splits each matrix multiplication and all-reduces activations four times per layer for every microbatch, so it belongs inside a server on the fastest links; **pipeline parallelism** splits the layers into stages that pass only boundary activations, so it can cross servers, but it leaves stages idle while the pipeline fills and drains. With 4 stages and 4 microbatches, that idle time is 75% of the useful work and 43% of elapsed time; more microbatches shrink it.

Before changing a layout, measure where the step's time goes (the [running-at-scale post](/blog/model-development-running-at-scale/) shows how): a split that relieves memory can still lose if its communication lands on slow links. [Data parallelism](/blog/model-development-data-parallelism/) and [sharding](/blog/model-development-sharding-recomputation/) are the background.

## Tensor parallelism: splitting a matrix multiplication

The feed-forward network computes $Z = \text{GeLU}(XA)\,B$, with $X$ the token representations, $A$ expanding the width and $B$ projecting back. **Tensor parallelism** splits both matrices across $t$ GPUs ([Shoeybi et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.08053); [Narayanan et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.04473), §2.3):

1. Split $A$ **by columns**, $A = [A_1, A_2]$. Each GPU computes $\text{GeLU}(XA_i)$ for its own columns. No communication is needed, because the activation applies to each column independently.
2. Split $B$ **by rows**, matching. Each GPU computes $\text{GeLU}(XA_i)\,B_i$, a partial result of full width.
3. **All-reduce** the partial results: their sum is exactly $Z$.

The order matters. Splitting $A$ by rows instead would leave each GPU with part of a sum *inside* the activation, and $\text{GeLU}(u + v) \neq \text{GeLU}(u) + \text{GeLU}(v)$. Running both versions on two processes makes the point: the column-then-row split matches the unsplit layer to within $4 \times 10^{-6}$, while the row split is wrong by up to 2.55.

Attention splits the same way, with each GPU taking whole heads. So each layer needs one all-reduce after attention and one after the MLP in the forward pass, and two more in the backward pass (Narayanan et al., §2.3). Those all-reduces carry activations, $b \times s \times h$ values: for one sequence of 4,096 tokens at width 8,192, about 67 MB in BF16, four times per layer for every microbatch. That frequency is why tensor parallelism needs very fast links.

## Pipeline parallelism: splitting the layers

**Pipeline parallelism** gives each of $p$ GPUs a contiguous group of layers, a **stage**. A microbatch flows through stage 1, then 2, and so on, and its gradients flow back. Communication is light, only the activations at stage boundaries, but there is an obvious problem: while stage 1 works on the first microbatch, stages 2 to $p$ wait.

Splitting the batch into $m$ microbatches lets stages work on different microbatches at once, but filling and draining the pipeline still leaves idle time, the **bubble**. With forward and backward times $t_f$ and $t_b$ per microbatch per stage, the bubble lasts $(p-1)(t_f + t_b)$ against useful work of $m(t_f + t_b)$ (Narayanan et al., §2.2.1):

$$
\text{bubble overhead} = \frac{p - 1}{m}\ \text{(idle time relative to useful work)}, \qquad \text{idle share of elapsed time} = \frac{p - 1}{m + p - 1}.
$$

The paper's "bubble fraction" is the first: overhead measured against useful work. With 4 stages and 4 microbatches, a simplified schedule gives each stage 4 work slots and 3 idle slots: an overhead of $3/4 = 75\%$, and $3/7 \approx 43\%$ of elapsed time spent idle. With 64 microbatches, the overhead is 4.7% and the idle share 4.5%; the two measures converge as the bubble shrinks. Many microbatches make the bubble small, but in the original GPipe schedule every microbatch's activations stay in memory until its backward pass. The **1F1B** schedule fixes that: after a warm-up, each stage alternates one forward and one backward pass, so at most $p$ microbatches are in flight. **Interleaving** shrinks the bubble further at the cost of more communication; the arithmetic is in the [optional section](#optional-interleaved-schedules).

## Sequence and context parallelism

Tensor parallelism splits the matrix multiplications but leaves some operations, the norms and dropout, replicated on every GPU, together with their activations. **Sequence parallelism** splits those operations along the sequence dimension instead. Replacing each all-reduce with a reduce-scatter and an all-gather moves the data between the two layouts with no extra communication ([Korthikanti et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.05198), §4.2.2).

For very long sequences even that is not enough: the attention itself, every token against every other, has to be split. **Context parallelism** gives each GPU a block of the sequence. Each GPU computes attention for its queries against its own keys and values, then passes its key-value block to the next GPU in a ring while receiving another, until every query has seen every key ([Liu et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01889)). The communication overlaps with computation, and memory per GPU falls with the number of GPUs.

## Expert parallelism

In a [mixture-of-experts model](/blog/model-development-decoder-architecture/#mixture-of-experts), different GPUs hold different experts. Each token must travel to the GPUs holding its chosen experts and back: two **all-to-all** exchanges per MoE layer, one to dispatch tokens and one to combine results. DeepSeek-V3 trained with 64-way expert parallelism spanning 8 nodes, combined with 16-way pipeline parallelism and ZeRO-1 data parallelism, and no tensor parallelism at all; it relied on custom all-to-all kernels and on overlapping communication with computation to hide the cost ([DeepSeek-V3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437), §3.2). Load balance is the operational hazard: if the router sends too many tokens to one expert, its GPU becomes the bottleneck.

## Putting them together

Real runs combine data, tensor, pipeline and sometimes context and expert parallelism, and the product of the degrees equals the number of GPUs. Each has its own communication pattern:

| Split | Communicates | How often |
|---|---|---|
| Data (with ZeRO) | gradients, gathered weights | gradients once per optimizer step if accumulation skips synchronization; stage 3 also gathers weights per layer for every microbatch |
| Tensor | activations, all-reduce | 4 times per layer for every microbatch |
| Pipeline | activations at stage boundaries | per microbatch, point to point |
| Context | key-value blocks around a ring | per attention layer |
| Expert | tokens, all-to-all | 2 per MoE layer, each direction |

Narayanan et al.'s first rule of thumb follows from that table: use tensor parallelism up to the number of GPUs in a server, where links are fastest, and pipeline parallelism to scale across servers (§3.2, Takeaway #1). Data parallelism then multiplies throughput, ideally with as little model parallelism as needed to fit.


**Try it: size a bubble.** A model is split into 16 pipeline stages, and the global batch gives each pipeline 32 microbatches. How large is the bubble overhead, and what share of elapsed time is idle?

**Answer.** Overhead $(16-1)/32 \approx$ **0.47**: nearly half as much idle time as useful work, so $15/47 \approx$ **32%** of elapsed time is idle, a poor configuration. The remedies are more microbatches (a larger global batch, which may not be wanted), fewer stages (more tensor parallelism or sharding instead), or both.


## Optional: interleaved schedules

With interleaving, each GPU holds $v$ smaller, non-contiguous chunks of layers instead of one contiguous stage, which shrinks the bubble overhead to $\frac{1}{v}\cdot\frac{p-1}{m}$ at the cost of $v$ times as many stage-boundary transfers (Narayanan et al., §2.2.2). In the exercise above, 4 chunks per GPU bring the overhead from 0.47 to $0.47/4 \approx 0.12$ and the idle share of elapsed time from 32% to $3.75/35.75 \approx 10\%$. The [Megatron-LM review](/paper-reviews/megatron-ptd-p/) shows that the benefit is largest at small batch sizes and shrinks as the batch grows.
